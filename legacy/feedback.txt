As it stands, this feels like a **good workshop / tech-report / artifact paper**, but **not yet a strong top-conference paper**. The core reason is that the paper mostly says **“here is a useful simulator with a grounded WiFi model, and here are some sanity checks plus a few illustrative studies”** rather than **“here is a new scientific result or design insight that required this simulator, and here is strong evidence for it.”** 

The strongest parts are already there: the problem is real, the architecture is clean, the WiFi model is more grounded than the usual edge/fog simulators, and the current experiments do show that wireless interference can dramatically change conclusions about makespan and routing. In particular, the paper clearly positions itself as bridging DAG scheduling and wireless-network effects, and it reports very large slowdowns when contention is modeled, including the 4x4 cases where interference changes makespan by more than an order of magnitude. 

What is missing is **depth of evidence** and **a sharper scientific claim**.

## The main gap

A top conference will usually ask something like:

* What is the **new insight** about networked computing, not just the new tool?
* Why is this not just a careful engineering integration of known ingredients?
* How do I know the simulator is **useful enough and faithful enough** to support new conclusions?
* What algorithmic or systems conclusion becomes visible only because of ncsim?

Right now the paper’s contributions are mostly: modular simulator, grounded 802.11 model, routing support, verification against analytical predictions, and open-source implementation. Those are solid, but they read more like a strong artifact/demo/toolkit contribution than a flagship conference contribution. 

## What would make it much stronger

### 1. Add a real scientific thesis, not just a tool thesis

The paper needs one or two central claims of the form:

* **Ignoring wireless contention systematically misranks schedulers/routing policies.**
* **Classical DAG schedulers like HEFT become badly miscalibrated in multi-hop WiFi settings.**
* **Routing decisions that look good under static bandwidth models become harmful once interference domains are considered.**
* **A lightweight flow-level but physically grounded model is sufficient to reproduce the ranking changes seen in a higher-fidelity reference model.**

You already hint at this with the routing and interference tables, especially the cases where shortest-path beats widest-path and where interference creates 1000%+ slowdowns. But that currently appears as an example, not as the paper’s main theorem-like message. 

For a 12-page conference paper, I would restructure around something like:

1. **Question:** When do traditional task schedulers fail in wireless networked computing?
2. **Method:** ncsim provides the missing simulation middle ground.
3. **Finding 1:** Network-unaware scheduling can catastrophically misestimate makespan.
4. **Finding 2:** Route selection and compute placement interact nontrivially through interference domains.
5. **Finding 3:** Lightweight grounded simulation is enough to preserve these ranking changes.

That turns the simulator into the enabler rather than the endpoint.

### 2. Validate against something external, not only against your own equations

This is probably the single biggest weakness today.

The WiFi validation is mostly **simulator vs analytical model that the simulator itself implements**. That is useful for checking correctness, but a reviewer may say: “You showed internal consistency, not realism.” The paper itself acknowledges that packet-level stacks and dynamic channels are omitted. 

To get much stronger, add at least one of these:

* **ns-3 comparison** on a carefully chosen subset of small topologies
* **small real testbed comparison** with a few laptops or embedded nodes over WiFi
* **comparison against published measurements** for hidden-terminal/contention regimes

The key is not perfect matching. The key is to show that ncsim preserves:

* throughput regime transitions,
* scheduler/routing ranking,
* relative slowdown trends,
* critical thresholds.

Even a 3-figure external validation section would materially improve the paper.

### 3. Show that ncsim changes algorithmic conclusions, not just absolute numbers

Right now the evaluation shows shortest vs widest and interference on/off. Good start. But top-conference strength comes from showing that **policy ranking changes**.

What I would add:

* Compare **HEFT/CPOP/network-unaware variants/manual baselines** under:

  * no interference,
  * simple static sharing,
  * csma_bianchi.
* Show how often the “best” scheduler under a naive model is **not** the best under grounded wireless modeling.
* Quantify rank inversions:

  * percentage of scenarios where winner changes,
  * median regret from choosing the wrong model,
  * worst-case regret.

This would turn the paper from “the simulator produces large effects” into “the simulator reveals wrong algorithmic choices under common abstractions.”

### 5. Expand scale and breadth of evaluation

The current scale tops out at 16 nodes and 20-task DAGs, with runtimes “on the order of seconds.” 

That is fine for a first version, but for a top conference it feels small. You do not necessarily need huge scale, but you do need broader experimental coverage:

* more topology families: line, tree, random geometric, clustered, corridor, asymmetric mesh
* more DAG families: chains, forks, joins, wide layers, communication-heavy vs compute-heavy
* sensitivity sweeps:

  * path loss exponent,
  * carrier-sense threshold,
  * task/data skew,
  * node heterogeneity,
  * DAG arrival rate / multiple workflows
* more metrics:

  * makespan,
  * queueing delay,
  * fairness,
  * communication/computation overlap,
  * fraction of time in contention-limited regime,
  * route stretch,
  * scheduler runtime

A paper becomes much stronger when the results generalize beyond one neat set of grids.

### 6. Quantify where the model is accurate enough and where it breaks

The paper honestly lists tradeoffs: no packet-level simulation, no fading, no mobility, single-slot compute, static topology. That honesty is good. 

But a top paper should go one step further and answer:

* In what operating region is ncsim trustworthy?
* What phenomena does it intentionally not capture?
* Which conclusions are robust despite those omissions?

A simple “scope of validity” figure/table would help a lot:

* low/moderate mobility: not supported
* static topologies with bulk transfers: well supported
* 802.11 contention/hidden terminals: supported
* TCP dynamics/fairness pathologies: not modeled
* fine PHY fluctuations: approximated only

That both strengthens credibility and defuses reviewer attacks.

### 7. Strengthen the related-work positioning

The current related-work section is reasonable but still somewhat generic: ns-3/OMNeT++, SimGrid/WRENCH/CloudSim, iFogSim/EdgeCloudSim. 

To be stronger, the related work should sharpen the gap along **three dimensions simultaneously**:

* workflow/DAG scheduling support,
* multi-hop wireless contention realism,
* speed/lightweight usability for large sweeps.

A compact comparison table would help:
columns like

* DAG-native?
* multi-hop wireless?
* CSMA contention?
* hidden terminals?
* packet-level?
* runtime cost / usability
* intended use case

That would make the niche visually undeniable.

### 8. The current evaluation section needs one “headline figure”

Right now the tables have useful numbers, but there is no single unforgettable figure.

You want one plot that makes a reviewer immediately think:
“Ah, this matters.”

Examples:

* **Policy-rank inversion heatmap** across topology × DAG × interference model
* **Prediction error of naive model** versus grounded model
* **Makespan regret from ignoring interference** across many random instances <<< MAKESPAN REGRET from ignoring interference could be the KEY NEW IDEA/CONTRIBUTION 
* **Scatter: naive estimate vs ncsim estimate**, showing systematic underestimation

That kind of figure is often what turns a paper from “competent” into “memorable.”

## A stronger paper structure

Here is the structure I would recommend for a 12-page submission:

1. **Introduction**

   * motivating failure case
   * sharp claims
   * contributions framed as scientific + systems

2. **Problem and gap**

   * why existing simulators are either too heavy or too abstract
   * why wireless network effects matter for DAG scheduling

3. **ncsim model**

   * enough detail to understand abstraction level
   * clearly state assumptions and scope

4. **Validation**

   * internal analytical checks
   * external validation versus ns-3 or testbed

5. **Case study: when naive models fail**

   * show ranking inversions and regret

7. **Large evaluation**

   * broad sweep and takeaways

8. **Limitations and reproducibility**

   * honest scope

9. **Conclusion**

## If you only had time to add three things

I would prioritize these, in order:

First, **external validation** against ns-3 or a small testbed.

Second, **a broader experiment showing rank inversions/regret**, not just a few illustrative tables.
