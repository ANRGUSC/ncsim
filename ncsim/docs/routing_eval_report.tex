\section{Experiment Setup}

\textbf{Simulator:} ncsim, HEFT scheduler, \texttt{csma\_bianchi} 802.11ax interference model.
\textbf{RF config:} 5\,GHz, 20\,MHz channel, $P_{\mathrm{tx}}=20$\,dBm, path-loss exponent $n=3.0$.
\textbf{Grid spacing:} 40\,m (adjacent links $\approx8.6$\,MB/s PHY; diagonals $\approx56.6$\,m, lower PHY rate).
\textbf{Seeds:} Each configuration averaged over 30 seeds (smoothing HEFT non-determinism from Python hash randomization).
\textbf{Task config:} \texttt{compute\_cost}${}=500$, \texttt{data\_size}${}=10$\,MB per edge.

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Static Greedy (GS) Dominates on the $4\times4$ Grid}

On the smaller $4\times4$ grid, all four greedy variants (GS/GC/GB/GO)
dramatically outperform the baselines, cutting makespan by 51--61\%.  GS is
consistently the best of the four, though GC is within 1--3\%.  The margin
among greedy variants is small because with only 16 nodes and 40\,m spacing,
most node pairs are 1--2 hops apart, and there are fewer alternative paths to
differentiate the orderings.

\textbf{GSD underperforms on $4\times4$ large (374.8\,s vs GS 201.3\,s).}
This is the most striking result.  GSD's per-flow greedy optimization picks
the best path for \emph{each individual transfer} at start time, but cannot
coordinate across flows.  On a small, dense grid where many transfers share
the same few links, the static planner's global throughput objective produces
better overall link utilization.  GSD ends up making locally optimal but
globally suboptimal choices that cascade through the multi-stage pipeline.

\subsection{Shortest-Path Wins on $7\times7$ with a Small DAG}

With only 8 tasks on 49 nodes, HEFT spreads tasks across the grid, creating
long-distance transfers.  Shortest-Path's min-hop paths keep transfers on
1--2 hop routes where possible, avoiding the multi-hop relay chains that
Widest-Path selects (which traverse more links and trigger more
interference).

The greedy variants (GS${}=249$\,s, GO${}=225$\,s, GSD${}=167$\,s) all lose
to S ($110$\,s) here.  The static greedy planners suffer because their HEFT
timing estimates are inaccurate for such a sparse DAG on a large grid---the
concurrency windows they compute don't match reality.  GSD does better
(167\,s) because it sees actual state, but still can't beat S's simple
min-hop strategy when there are only 12 transfers and 240 links to spread
across.

\subsection{GSD Wins on $7\times7$ with a Large DAG}

The 60-task pipeline on the $7\times7$ grid is the most complex scenario,
with 102 edges creating sustained concurrent traffic across the network.
Here GSD (3447\,s) edges out GB (3541\,s) by 2.7\%.  The real-time state
information becomes valuable: with many active transfers, GSD can route each
new flow around current hot spots.

The static greedy variants (GS${}=3583$\,s, GC${}=3618$\,s, GB${}=3541$\,s,
GO${}=3619$\,s) are all close, with GB slightly ahead---suggesting that
prioritizing large transfers in the greedy ordering matters less when
transfer sizes are uniform (here all 10\,MB, so GB degenerates to an
arbitrary tie-break that happens to produce slightly better routes for this
topology).

\subsection{Greedy Ordering Matters Less Than Expected}

Across all four experiments, the four greedy orderings produce makespans
within 2--12\% of each other.  The benchmark uses uniform
\texttt{data\_size}${}=10$\,MB, which neutralizes GB's advantage, and equal
\texttt{compute\_cost}${}=500$, which reduces the dynamic range of
criticality.  GO's overlap-degree sorting helps on $7\times7$ small
(225\,s vs GS 249\,s, ${-}10\%$) where temporal overlap estimation is more
predictive.

In workloads with heterogeneous transfer sizes and compute costs, we expect
the orderings to diverge more.

\subsection{Widest-Path Is Consistently the Worst}

W never wins and is $2$--$14\times$ worse than the best scheme.  Its paths
maximize raw bandwidth but ignore interference, often selecting long relay
chains that traverse many links and trigger severe contention.  In wireless
mesh networks under \texttt{csma\_bianchi}, path length and interference
matter far more than nominal link bandwidth.

\section{Implications for Deferral}

The results suggest deferral would be most valuable in exactly the regime
where GSD already wins (large grid, large DAG):

\begin{itemize}\item \textbf{On $4\times4$ grids}, the network is too small for deferral to
      help---all paths are short and there are limited alternatives.  The
      bottleneck is coordinating routes, which static GS handles well.

\item \textbf{On $7\times7$ with small DAGs}, transfers are infrequent
      enough that contention is mild.  Deferral would add latency without
      benefit.

\item \textbf{On $7\times7$ with large DAGs}, there are many concurrent
      transfers competing for airtime.  A deferred transfer that waits 1--2
      transfer-completions might find a much less congested path.  The risk is
      stalling critical-path tasks.  A conservative deferral policy (defer
      only non-critical transfers when current best-path bandwidth is below,
      say, 30\% of the uncongested baseline) could improve on GSD's 3447\,s
      without the starvation risks of aggressive deferral.
\end{itemize}
